General Clinical Examination
Complete Head-to-Tail Protocol
▾
Exam Order (Always Follow)
1. Signalment & History
Age, breed, sex, reproductive status, chief complaint
2. General Appearance (GA)
Observe from distance before handling · Mentation, BCS, posture, gait
3. Vital Signs (TPR)
Weight, temperature, pulse, respiration, MM/CRT
4. Systems Exam (Head to Tail)
EENT → CV → RESP → ABD → GU → PLN → INTEG → M/S → NERV
TPR Normal Ranges
Temperature
Dog: 38–39.2°C (100.5–102.5°F) · Cat: 38–39.2°C · <99°F or >105°F → SUPERVISOR!
Pulse (femoral artery)
Dog: 80–160 bpm · Cat: 110–200 bpm · Check strength (strong/weak/thready/bounding)
Respiration
Dog: 15–30 breaths/min · Cat: 20–40 breaths/min · Count visually or by auscultation
Capillary Refill Time (CRT)
Normal < 2 sec · Press gum, release, count seconds to return to pink
Dehydration Assessment
0–5% – Adequate
Skin snaps back immediately · CRT normal · MM pink and moist
5–8% – Marginal
Slight delay 2–4 sec skin return · CRT ~2 sec · Eyes slightly sunken · MM slightly dry
8–10% – Obvious
Delay 5–10 sec skin return · CRT 2–2.5 sec · Eyes sunken · MM dry and tacky
10–12% – Severe
Skin stays tented 10–30 sec · CRT 3+ sec · Very sunken eyes · Depression, signs of shock
12–15% – Inadequate
Shock state · Death probable → IV FLUIDS EMERGENCY
Mucous Membrane Colors
Pink
Normal perfusion and oxygenation
Pale/White
Anemia, poor perfusion, shock, blood loss, vasoconstriction
Blue (Cyanotic)
Inadequate oxygenation (hypoxemia) → EMERGENCY
Brick Red
Vasodilation – early shock, sepsis, fever, SIRS
Yellow (Icteric)
Bilirubin accumulation – hepatic/biliary disorder or hemolysis
Brown
Methemoglobinemia – acetaminophen toxicity in cats!
Petechiae (red spots)
Coagulation disorder – platelet disorder, DIC, coagulation factor deficiency
EENT (Eyes, Ears, Nose, Throat)
Eyes
Size, position, discharge · Lids, conjunctiva, sclera, pupil (size/symmetry), cornea (clarity), lens · Note squinting, cloudiness, redness
Ears
Carriage, position · Thickness/malleability of pinnae · Cleanliness of ear canals · Odor? Discharge?
Nose & Nares
Symmetry, conformation, discharge (serous/purulent/hemorrhagic/mucoid)
Mouth
Lips, MM color, teeth, hard/soft palate, tongue, pharynx, tonsils · Note odor, gingivitis, calculus
Lymph Nodes (submandibular)
Palpate under jaw · Should be firm and freely moveable
CV (Cardiovascular) Auscultation
Cardiac Auscultation Sites
Left 4th–6th intercostal space (PMI/mitral) · Left 2nd–4th (pulmonic) · Left 3rd–5th mid-thorax (aortic) · Right 3rd–5th mid-thorax (tricuspid)
Normal Heart Sounds
"Lub-Dub" · S1 (loud, long, low) = AV valve closure · S2 = semilunar valve closure
Abnormal Findings
Arrhythmia · Murmur (swooshing sound) · Muffled sounds (fluid in chest?) · Gallop rhythm
Heart Murmur Grading
1/6 = very soft · 2/6 = soft · 3/6 = moderate · 4/6 = loud · 5/6 = loud with thrill · 6/6 = heard off chest
RESP (Respiratory) Auscultation
Normal Sounds
Vesicular/bronchial – soft, breezy, rustling sounds
Wheezes
High-pitched hissing on expiration – small airway disease (asthma)
Rales/Crackles
Discontinuous, brief sounds on inspiration – fluid in lungs
Dull Sounds
Pneumonia or consolidation
Absent Sounds
Pleural effusion or space-occupying lesion
Respiratory Localization: Loud breathing = large airway (nose/trachea/larynx) · Inspiratory noise = extrathoracic (larynx) · Expiratory noise = intrathoracic trachea · Rapid/shallow = pleural space disease
ABD (Abdomen) Palpation
Technique
1–2 hands, start at spine, move ventrally · Allow viscera to slip through fingers · Note organ size, location, fluid, gas, masses, pain
Cranial Abdomen
Stomach, liver, spleen, pancreas area, small intestine
Mid-Abdomen
Spleen, kidneys, small intestine
Caudal Abdomen
Urinary bladder, prostate, uterus, colon, small intestine
Key Organs
Liver: sharp edges (usually non-palpable) · Kidneys: right more cranial than left (non-palpable in dogs) · Bladder: pear-shaped (dog), spherical (cat)
GU (Genitourinary) & Perineum
Always Verify
Sex and reproductive status – don't trust client info!
Perianal Area
Hair mats, hernias, feces, masses, discharge · Palpate anal sacs (dogs)
Male
Prepuce/penis: discharge, inflammation, tumors · Expose penis, check for trauma · Testicles: symmetry, size, both descended?
Female
Mammary glands: tumors, cysts, swelling, heat, discharge · Vulva: size, inflammation, discharge (blood/pus), polyps, tumors

Palpate
Submandibular, prescapular, axillary, inguinal, popliteal

Firm, freely moveable · Usually palpable: submandibular, prescapular, popliteal, inguinal
Abnormal

Alopecia, masses, parasites (fleas/ticks/lice), dryness, excessive oil, matting
Limbs & Joints
Palpate each limb/joint · Note swelling, pain, deformities, fractures, luxations, ROM, atrophy, crepitus · Assess weight-bearing
Feet & Nails
Check condition, trim if needed
Vertebral Column
Palpate for deviations and pain

Step-by-Step Diagnostic Approach
RULE OF THUMB: Always rule out the 3 major causes first!
1. Ectoparasites (fleas, scabies, Cheyletiella, Demodex)
2. Infections (bacterial pyoderma, Malassezia, dermatophytosis)
3. Allergies (flea, food, environmental/atopic)
1. Rule Out Ectoparasites
Flea allergic dermatitis (FAD)
Dorsal rump itch · Flea dirt (black specks) · Erythema, papules, hair loss · Treat ALL in-contact animals + environment!
Sarcoptes (scabies)
HIGHLY pruritic! · Pinnal-pedal reflex positive (scratch ear = hind leg scratches) · Skin scrapes (low sensitivity ~50%) · Contagious to humans!
Trial treatment = best test
Cheyletiella ("walking dandruff")
Dorsal scaling · Mild-moderate itch · Visible mites on tape prep · Contagious
Demodex
Face + paws · Usually NOT pruritic unless secondary pyoderma · Skin scrape to depth (capillary ooze)
Test: Coat brushing + tape prep (unstained) for fleas/Cheyletiella · Deep skin scrape for Sarcoptes/Demodex · Fecal flotation (cats) for Notoedres
2. Rule Out Infections
Bacterial pyoderma (Staphylococcus)
Papules, pustules, collarettes · Erythema · Moderate-severe itch · Cytology: cocci + neutrophils
Malassezia dermatitis (yeast)
Greasy, smelly skin · Ventral/flexural areas · Cytology: budding yeasts (peanut-shaped)
Dermatophytosis (ringworm)
Hair loss, scaling, crusts · Often NOT pruritic unless secondary infection · Wood's lamp (50% M. canis glow) · Fungal culture gold standard
Test: Impression smear or tape prep (stain with Diff-Quick) → look for cocci (bacteria), yeasts, inflammatory cells · DTM culture for ringworm
3. If Above Ruled Out → Allergic Dermatitis
Flea allergy dermatitis (FAD)
Strict flea control for 3 months (ALL animals + environment) · Use quality products (fipronil, selamectin, fluralaner)
Food allergy
Novel protein diet trial (single protein + carb) for 8–12 weeks · No treats, no flavored meds! · If improves → food challenge to confirm
Atopic dermatitis (environmental allergy)
Diagnosis of exclusion · Face, paws, ventral itch · Usually seasonal initially, becomes year-round · Steroid-responsive
Key Clue for Allergies: Face, paws, axillae/groin distribution + good steroid response + no parasites/infection found = allergic dermatitis

⚡ Shock Recognition & Types ▸
What is Shock?
CRITICAL DEFINITION: Shock = inadequate perfusion of tissues → inadequate delivery of oxygen and nutrients → inadequate elimination of metabolic byproducts
Common derangement
ALL forms of shock → inadequate tissue perfusion → cell death if not corrected
Three Main Types of Shock
1. HYPOVOLEMIC SHOCK
Most common in small animals · Blood/fluid loss · Dehydration
MOST COMMON
2. CARDIOGENIC SHOCK
Heart failure · Inadequate cardiac output · Arrhythmias
3. DISTRIBUTIVE SHOCK
Sepsis, anaphylaxis · Vasodilation · Maldistribution of blood flow
Clinical Recognition - Key Signs
Heart Rate
Usually INCREASED (150-200+ dogs, 200-260+ cats) · Tachycardia in most cases · Bradycardia = late, severe sign!
Pulse Quality
WEAK, thready, or bounding · Poor peripheral pulses
KEY SIGN!
Mucous Membranes (MM)
Pale, brick red (distributive), or cyanotic · Capillary refill time (CRT) >2 seconds
Temperature
Cold extremities · Decreased rectal temp (early septic may have increased)
Respiratory Rate
Increased (compensatory) · May be rapid/shallow · Anxiety
Mentation
Dull, depressed, anxious · Weakness · Collapse
Hypovolemic Shock - Specifics
Causes
Hemorrhage (trauma, surgery, GI bleeding) · Dehydration (vomiting, diarrhea, polyuria)
Signs
Pale MM · Weak pulses · Tachycardia · Cold extremities · Prolonged CRT
Treatment priority
VOLUME RESUSCITATION with fluids · Stop hemorrhage if ongoing
Distributive Shock (Septic) - Specifics
Causes
Sepsis, SIRS, anaphylaxis · Widespread vasodilation
Early signs ("warm shock")
BRICK RED MM · Bounding pulses · Hyperdynamic · Hyperthermia
Late signs ("cold shock")
Pale MM · Weak pulses · Hypothermia · Looks like hypovolemic
Warning: Distributive shock from sepsis can LOOK like hypovolemic! BUT aggressive fluid therapy can be LETHAL in cardiogenic shock!
Cardiogenic Shock - Specifics
Causes
Heart failure, arrhythmias, cardiac tamponade · Heart can't pump effectively
Signs
Weak pulses · Pale/cyanotic MM · Pulmonary edema · Jugular distension · Arrhythmias
CRITICAL WARNING
DO NOT give aggressive fluids! Will worsen heart failure · Give cautiously
⚠️ DANGER!
⚠️ Differentiate cardiogenic from hypovolemic BEFORE fluid bolus! Check for heart murmur, gallop, arrhythmia, pulmonary crackles
💧 Shock Treatment & Fluid Therapy ▸
Goals of Treatment
Improve perfusion and oxygen delivery
Restore blood volume · Improve cardiac output
Prevent complications
Hypoglycemia, hypercarbia, lactic acidosis, organ failure
Treat underlying cause
Stop hemorrhage, control infection, address primary problem
ABC Approach to Shock
Airway
Ensure patent airway · Intubate if needed
Breathing
Supplemental oxygen · Positive pressure ventilation if needed
Circulation
Fluid resuscitation · Control hemorrhage · Improve cardiac output
Fluid Therapy - Types of Fluids
CRYSTALLOIDS (first-line)
0.9% NaCl or Lactated Ringer's · Cheap, readily available · Volume expanders
FIRST CHOICE
COLLOIDS
Hetastarch, dextran · Stay in intravascular space longer · Use if crystalloids fail
BLOOD PRODUCTS
Whole blood, packed RBCs, plasma · For hemorrhagic shock with severe anemia
Crystalloid choice: 0.9% NaCl = isotonic, good for most · LRS = contains lactate (metabolized to bicarbonate), good for acidosis · Avoid dextrose fluids for volume resuscitation!
Shock Doses - Crystalloids
DOG shock dose
90 ml/kg (one "shock dose") · Give over 15-30 minutes
90 ml/kg
CAT shock dose
60 ml/kg (one "shock dose") · Give over 15-30 minutes
60 ml/kg
How to give
Give 1/4 to 1/2 shock dose as bolus → Reassess → Repeat if needed · Do NOT give entire dose at once!
Example: 20kg dog in shock → shock dose = 1800ml · Give 450ml bolus over 10-15 min → Reassess (HR, pulse, MM, CRT) → Repeat if still in shock
Fluid Resuscitation Protocol
Step 1: Assess severity
Is patient in shock? Mild, moderate, severe?
Step 2: IV catheter
Large bore peripheral (18-20G dogs, 22-24G cats) · Jugular if needed
Step 3: Give initial bolus
1/4 shock dose over 10-15 minutes (22.5 ml/kg dogs, 15 ml/kg cats)
Step 4: REASSESS!
HR, pulse quality, MM color, CRT, mentation · Check every 15 minutes
CRITICAL!
Step 5: Repeat if needed
Give another 1/4 shock dose if still in shock · Reassess again · Up to full shock dose
Step 6: Maintenance fluids
Once stabilized: 2-4 ml/kg/hr · Monitor urine output, hydration
2-4 ml/kg/hr
Monitoring Response to Treatment
Improving signs
HR decreases · Pulse quality improves · MM become pink · CRT normalizes · Mentation improves
Parameters to monitor
HR, RR, pulse quality, MM, CRT, temperature, BP, urine output, lactate
Urine output goal
1-2 ml/kg/hr = adequate renal perfusion
Warning signs of fluid overload: Increased RR, pulmonary crackles, chemosis, serous nasal discharge · STOP fluids!
Additional Supportive Care
Oxygen supplementation
Nasal O2, flow-by, oxygen cage · Improves tissue oxygenation
Keep warm
Blankets, warming pads · Hypothermia worsens shock
Glucose supplementation
Maintain blood glucose 90-110 mg/dl · Dextrose bolus/CRI if hypoglycemic
Treat underlying cause
Control hemorrhage · Antibiotics for sepsis · Surgery if needed
When to Refer
Not responding to fluids
Given full shock dose but still in shock · Needs ICU care
Cardiogenic shock
Needs specialized cardiac care · Echo, advanced monitoring
Ongoing hemorrhage
Needs surgery to control bleeding
Severe sepsis/septic shock
Needs intensive care, vasopressors, advanced support
🌡️ Fever: Assessment & Diagnosis ▸
What is Fever?
Definition
Elevation above normal body temperature from resetting the hypothalamic thermoregulatory set point
Normal temperatures
Dogs: 100-102.8°F (37.8-39.3°C) · Cats: 100-102.8°F (37.8-39.3°C)
Fever definition
Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO) = >103.5°F (>39.7°C) on ≥4 occasions over 14-day period
>103.5°F
Pathophysiology
Pyrogens (endogenous/exogenous) → release prostaglandins → reset hypothalamus to higher temperature
Fever vs Hyperthermia - KEY Difference!
CRITICAL: Fever and hyperthermia are DIFFERENT! Treatment differs!
FEVER
Hypothalamic set point RAISED · Body generates heat · Responds to antipyretics (NSAIDs)
HYPERTHERMIA
Hypothalamic set point NORMAL · Excessive heat/inability to cool · Does NOT respond to antipyretics!
DIFFERENT!
Hyperthermia causes: Heat stroke, seizures, excessive exercise · Temp >104-107°F · Treat with cooling, NOT drugs!
Clinical Signs of Fever
General signs
Lethargy · Inappetence · Depression · Tachycardia · Hyperpnea · Dehydration
Physical exam
Hot to touch · May have shivering/shaking · Check ears, paw pads
Temperature thresholds
103-105°F = moderate fever · >105°F = high fever (serious!) · >106°F = life-threatening
Most Common Causes of Fever
INFECTIOUS (MOST COMMON!)
Bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi · Abscesses, pneumonia, pyometra, sepsis
#1 CAUSE
Immune-mediated
IMHA, IMT, polyarthritis, vasculitis, SLE
Neoplasia
Lymphoma, leukemia, solid tumors · Especially with necrosis
Inflammatory
Pancreatitis, hepatitis, IBD, granulomatous disease
Drugs/toxins
Tetracycline, sulfonamides, penicillins, certain toxins
Diagnostic Approach to Fever
History
Duration, travel history, tick exposure, recent vaccination, drug administration
Physical exam
THOROUGH! Check lymph nodes, heart, lungs, abdomen, joints, oral cavity, rectal
Minimum database
CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis · Look for infection, inflammation, organ dysfunction
Additional tests (as indicated)
Blood cultures · Urine culture · FeLV/FIV · Tick titers · Imaging · Aspirates/biopsies
CBC/Biochemistry Findings
Leukocytosis
Left shift = bacterial infection likely · Neutrophilia common
Leukopenia
Overwhelming infection, bone marrow disease, parvovirus
Thrombocytopenia
Sepsis, tick-borne disease, DIC, immune-mediated
Elevated liver enzymes
Hepatitis, cholangiohepatitis, sepsis
💊 Fever Treatment ▸
Goals of Treatment
Goal: Reset thermoregulatory set point to lower level · Treat underlying cause · Support patient
When to treat fever
Only treat if: >105°F, patient life-threatened, impaired patient (heart/respiratory disease), or topical cooling unsuccessful
Fever may be HELPFUL! Suppresses bacterial replication, facilitates immune response · Don't treat mild fevers unless necessary
Supportive Care
Rest activity
Reduce metabolic demands
Hydration
Febrile patients lose water · Fluid therapy (IV or SC) essential
Nutrition
High caloric intake needed · Hypercatabolic state
Topical cooling
Use for very high fever (>106°F) · Fans, cool water, ice packs (avoid extremes)

😤 Cough ▸
What is a Cough?
Definition
Sudden forceful expiration of air through glottis · Preceded by exaggerated inspiratory effort · Protective mechanism
Mechanism
Stimulation of irritant receptors in pharynx/larynx/trachea/bronchi → vagus nerve → cough reflex
Types of Cough - Clues to Cause
Nocturnal cough
Left-sided CHF · Tracheal collapse · Pulmonary edema
Exercise-induced cough
Early CHF · Tracheal collapse · Laryngeal disease
Harsh, dry "honking" cough
Tracheal collapse (toy breeds) · Laryngeal disease · Infrequent = upper airway
Moist, productive cough
Fluid/mucus in airways · Pneumonia · Pulmonary edema · Bronchitis
Soft cough
No mucus/fluid production · Inflammation/irritation of larynx/trachea/bronchi

💙 Cyanosis (EMERGENCY!) ▸
What is Cyanosis?
🚨 CYANOSIS = LIFE-THREATENING EMERGENCY! Bluish discoloration of skin/mucous membranes · Severe hypoxia · Act immediately!
Definition
Bluish discoloration from increased deoxygenated hemoglobin in blood (>5 g/dl)
Pathophysiology
Central = arterial hypoxemia · Peripheral = venous blood pooling/poor circulation
Central vs Peripheral Cyanosis
Central cyanosis
Mucous membranes blue (tongue, gums) · Entire body · Low arterial oxygen · SEVERE!
CRITICAL!
Peripheral cyanosis
Only extremities (paw pads, ears, tail) · Poor circulation · Central oxygen OK
Check mucous membranes! If MM are blue = central cyanosis = EMERGENCY!
Causes - Central Cyanosis
Upper airway obstruction
Laryngeal paralysis, brachycephalic syndrome, foreign body, tracheal collapse (severe)
Lower airway disease
Severe pneumonia, pulmonary edema, pleural effusion, pneumothorax
Ventilation-perfusion mismatch
Pulmonary thromboembolism, severe heart disease
Right-to-left cardiac shunt
Congenital heart defects · Tetralogy of Fallot · Reverse PDA
Clinical Signs
Blue/purple MM
Tongue, gums · Most obvious sign
Severe dyspnea
Labored breathing · Open mouth · Extended neck
Weakness/collapse
Cannot stand · Syncope possible
Abnormal lung sounds
Stridor, crackles, wheezes, muffled · Depends on cause
IMMEDIATE Management
⚠️ OXYGEN NOW! This is a life-threatening emergency · Stabilize BEFORE diagnostics!
Supplemental oxygen
Flow-by, oxygen cage, mask · HIGHEST priority!
DO THIS FIRST!
Minimize stress
NO restraint · Quiet environment · Gentle handling · Sedation if very stressed
Emergency treatments
Thoracocentesis (if effusion) · Bronchodilators (if bronchoconstriction) · Furosemide (if CHF)
Intubation if needed
Complete airway obstruction · Severe laryngeal paralysis · Emergency tracheostomy
Diagnostics (AFTER Stabilization!)
Thoracic radiographs
ONLY after patient stabilized · Look for effusion, pneumothorax, masses, pneumonia
Arterial blood gas
Measures PaO2 directly · Confirms hypoxemia

If cardiac shunt suspected

ALL cyanotic patients
Need emergency care · Refer to ER/ICU if not responding to oxygen

Vomiting vs Regurgitation - KEY Differences
VOMITING
Active process · Retching · Abdominal contractions · Digested food/bile · AFTER eating (30+ min)
REGURGITATION
Passive · No retching · No effort · Undigested food · SOON after eating (<30 min) · Tube/sausage shape
Ask owner: "Does the dog retch/heave before bringing up food?" YES = vomit, NO = regurgitation

Mild (treat at home)
1-2 episodes · Eating well · Bright and alert · No blood · No systemic signs
Moderate (needs vet attention)
Vomiting >1 day · Depressed · Not eating · Dehydrated
Severe (URGENT!)
Blood in vomit · Abdominal pain · Bloated · Collapse · Toxin ingestion

🔍 Initial Assessment & Cleaning ▸
Initial Assessment - ABC First!
⚠️ CRITICAL: Accidental wounds are often part of more serious conditions (hit by car, respiratory injury, shock). Treat life-threatening problems FIRST before focusing on the wound!
Airway, Breathing, Circulation
Stabilize patient BEFORE wound care
Control hemorrhage
Direct pressure · Elevation · Pressure bandage
Assess wound severity
Depth, contamination, tissue viability, time since injury
The "Golden Period" - Timing Matters!
0-6 hours
OPTIMAL for primary closure · Minimal bacterial growth
6-12 hours
Still acceptable if clean wound · Consider delayed primary closure
>12 hours
HIGH infection risk · Usually second intention healing or delayed closure
Exception: Clean facial wounds (excellent blood supply) can often be closed up to 24 hours post-injury
Wound Cleaning - DO NOT Use These!
❌ Hydrogen peroxide
Damages tissue, delays healing, creates gas bubbles
❌ Strong antiseptics
Povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine AT FULL STRENGTH damage tissue
❌ Antibiotic sprays/powders
Tetracycline, neomycin sprays DON'T WORK and contaminate wound
❌ Alcohol
Extremely painful, damages tissue
These are TOXIC to healing tissues! They kill bacteria BUT also kill the cells needed for healing.
Wound Cleaning - What TO Use ✅
Sterile saline (0.9% NaCl)
BEST choice · Non-toxic · Effective mechanical cleaning
GOLD STANDARD
Dilute chlorhexidine (0.05%)
Dilute 1:40 from 2% stock · Antimicrobial + safe
0.05% solution
Dilute povidone-iodine (0.1-1%)
Dilute 1:10 to 1:100 · "Weak tea" color · Less ideal than above
Tap water (if nothing else)
Studies show tap water is safe for irrigation · Better than nothing!
Irrigation Technique
High-pressure lavage
35-60ml syringe + 18-20G needle · Creates 7-15 psi pressure
Essential!
Volume matters
Use COPIOUS amounts · 50-100ml per cm of wound minimum
Timing critical
Irrigation within 4 hours post-injury = VERY effective at removing bacteria
Remove foreign material
Dirt, debris, devitalized tissue · Essential for healing
Pro tip: Be careful with jet irrigation - avoid separating tissue planes and driving bacteria deeper!
Clipping & Preparation
Clip generously
Wide margin around wound · Use clippers, NOT scissors
Protect wound during clipping
Pack wound with sterile gauze or apply water-soluble gel first
Scrub AROUND wound
Use dilute chlorhexidine/betadine on surrounding skin · NOT in wound itself
🧵 Wound Closure Techniques ▸
Primary Closure (First Intention Healing)
When to use
Clean wound · <6 hours old · Minimal tissue loss · Can approximate edges without tension
Advantages
Fastest healing · Minimal scarring · Best cosmetic result · Protection from contamination
Requirements
Clean wound · Adequate blood supply · No tension · Dead space eliminated
Debridement - Critical First Step!
Remove ALL devitalized tissue
Necrotic tissue = bacterial growth medium · Must be excised
Sharp debridement
Scalpel, scissors · Create clean surgical margins · Minimal tissue sacrifice
Assess tissue viability
Color, bleeding, warmth · If questionable, debride it
Remove foreign material
Dirt, debris, embedded material · May need enlarging wound to explore
For crushing/avulsion wounds: Extensive debridement needed · Remove compromised tissue aggressively
Suturing Techniques
Eliminate dead space
Use buried sutures · Dead space = seroma/hematoma/infection risk
NO TENSION!
Excessive tension → sutures tear through tissue · Use tension-relieving sutures if needed
Critical!
Suture materials
Monofilament nylon for skin · Absorbable (PDS, Vicryl) for buried layers · 3-0 to 5-0 typical
Bandage after closure
Control edema · Immobilize · Absorb drainage · Protect sutures
Delayed Primary Closure
When to use
Contaminated wound · 3-5 days post-injury · Healthy granulation present
Technique
Clean wound daily · Wait 3-4 days for granulation · Then close with sutures
3-5 days
Advantages
Allows infection to declare itself · Better than primary closure of contaminated wound
Key point: Wound edges become thicker and stiffer after 3-4 days · May need excision before closure
Second Intention Healing (Open Wound)
When to use
Severe contamination · Extensive tissue loss · Cannot approximate edges · >12 hours old + dirty
Technique
Clean daily · Bandage with non-adherent dressing · Allow contraction + epithelialization
Bandaging
Gauze impregnated with Vaseline · Change q2-7 days · Keep moist but not wet
Timeline
Granulation 5-7 days · Contraction 1-3 weeks · Full healing 3-8+ weeks
When antibiotics needed
ONLY if bacterial proliferation + invasion + systemic signs · NOT routine!
Not always!
Large open wounds that can't be closed: Wash daily with saline · Bandage · May take weeks but heal surprisingly well!

Otoscopic Examination Technique
Step 1: Position patient
Lateral recumbancy · Affected ear UP
Step 2: Select appropriate cone
Largest cone that fits comfortably · Don't force
Step 3: Straighten ear canal
Pull pinna UP and AWAY from head (dorsal direction) · Straightens L-shaped canal
Step 4: Insert otoscope
Maintain traction on pinna · Look for canal crusting, hair, erythema, discharge
Step 5: Assess tympanic membrane
Color, health, integrity · CRUCIAL - if ruptured, certain meds are ototoxic!
CRITICAL: If tympanic membrane is NOT visible → assume it's damaged. DO NOT use potentially ototoxic cleaners/medications!
What to Look For - Ear Canal
Normal
Pink, hairless tissue · Minimal wax · Smooth walls
Erythema (redness)
Inflammation from infection, allergy, foreign body
Stenosis (narrowing)
Chronic inflammation → thickened canal walls → narrowed lumen
Discharge
Brown waxy = Malassezia · Yellow/green purulent = bacteria · Black granular = ear mites
Polyps/masses
Smooth growths (polyps) vs irregular (neoplasia)

🧼 Ear Cleaning Techniques ▸
WHY Ear Cleaning is Essential
Remove physical barriers
Wax, debris, pus block medication penetration
Antimicrobials inactivated
Debris and pus contain proteins that neutralize antibiotics
Remove inflammatory by-products
Degenerating cellular debris + metabolic waste perpetuate inflammation
Bottom line: Cleaning ears is NOT optional - it's essential for therapeutic response!
Ear Cleaning Technique - Dogs
Step 1: Restraint
Place dog on table chest-high · Drape arm over shoulders
Step 2: Wipe visible discharge
Use gauze (NOT Q-tips!) to remove surface debris
Step 3: Fill ear canal
Pull pinna UP · Gently fill with cleaning solution
Step 4: Massage base of ear
Massage VERY GENTLY · Hear squishing sound · Most dogs find this soothing
Step 5: Let dog shake
Release head · Dog shakes out debris/solution
Step 6: Wipe out debris
Use gauze to remove dislodged material
Step 7: Apply medication
After ear is relatively clean · Massage again to distribute
⚠️ NEVER use Q-tips! They push debris deeper and can rupture tympanum.
Ear Cleaning Technique - Cats
Step 1: "Burrito" wrap
Wrap cat in towel (only head/tail stick out)
Step 2: Hug cat gently
One hand under chin · Other hand free to work
Step 3-7: Same as dogs
Fill → massage → shake → wipe → medicate

Require 10-15 min contact time
10-15 min wait!
